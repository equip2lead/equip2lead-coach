NINE HABITS THAT ACTUALLY BUILD LEADERSHIP
(Original — for wherever fits: candidate for Module 9, or a standalone tool)

Leadership isn't a trait you either have or don't. It's closer to physical 
conditioning — built through specific, repeatable habits, not a single 
insight. Nine are worth naming.

1. Know what's actually happening inside you.
Leaders who last stay unusually honest with themselves about their own 
reactions — what triggers them, what they're avoiding, what they're 
actually feeling versus what they're performing. This isn't introspection 
for its own sake. It's the raw material everything else is built from.

2. Notice your emotions without being run by them.
Frustration and disappointment are normal. The difference between a 
leader who lasts and one who doesn't is rarely the absence of these 
feelings — it's whether the feeling makes the decision, or the leader 
does.

3. Ask for the feedback that actually stings.
Most people ask "how am I doing?" and mean "tell me I'm doing well." 
Leaders worth following ask a harder question — specifically what isn't 
working — and then say thank you instead of explaining why the 
feedback is wrong.

4. Move before you're told to.
Waiting for permission to solve an obvious problem is the single most 
common way capable people stay stuck at the level they're already at.

5. Let someone else see what you can't see in yourself.
Every leader who's kept growing past their first few years has had 
someone in their corner whose job was specifically to notice their blind 
spots — not a friend who's nice to them, someone who's honest with 
them.

6. Set a real target, not a vague hope.
"Get better at leading" isn't a goal. A goal has an edge you can actually 
measure yourself against.

7. Treat every rep as practice, including the failed ones.
Skill comes from repetition, and repetition includes the attempts that 
didn't work. Leaders who improve fastest are the ones who mine their 
failures for the lesson instead of just moving past them.

8. Check the scoreboard on purpose.
A goal with no way to track progress produces the same result as no 
goal at all — you find out you missed it only once it's too late to 
adjust.

9. Stay honest about your own mistakes, even when no one's watching 
closely.
The leaders people trust most aren't the ones who never get anything 
wrong. They're the ones who admit it plainly when they do, without 
waiting to be caught.

None of these nine require a title. All nine require the same thing: doing 
them on a day nobody's checking.
